S2 Text. Cross-protocol length bias: why the whole-cell versus single-nucleus comparison is uninformative for the covariance layer.

This note substantiates one sentence in Results §2, that comparing whole-cell to single-nucleus data confounds biology with assay and that in nuclear data the leading within-type covariance axis is dominated by transcript length rather than cell identity. It also sets out what the same-protocol primate replication does and does not control. It does not relitigate the covariance result.

Setup. Every cell entering the primary human and mouse centroids was prepared by a whole-cell protocol. The human side is 96.1% 10x Chromium (89.2% 3' v3 and 6.9% 5' v2), with the remaining 3.9% Smart-seq2 or Smart-seq3; the mouse side is 67.0% 10x Chromium and 33.0% Smart-seq2 (Methods). The PanSci atlas, by contrast, is EasySci single-nucleus RNA-seq. Applying the two-layer decomposition across that pair is therefore a whole-cell versus single-nucleus protocol switch rather than an independent same-protocol pair. Nuclear and cytoplasmic RNA sample different molecular pools: single-nucleus libraries are enriched for nascent, intron-retaining transcripts and deplete the mature, highly expressed cytoplasmic transcripts, ribosomal-protein mRNAs prominent among them, that dominate whole-cell covariance.

The Krzanowski statistic clears its null but the axis is not shared. On the PanSci pair the Layer-2 covariance-orientation statistic is significant both before and after the centroid-optimal rotation (pre-rotation k = 5: S = 0.396 versus null mean 0.302, p < 10⁻⁴; post-rotation k = 5: S = 0.402 versus null mean 0.360, p < 10⁻⁴). This is a cross-protocol sensitivity check rather than an independent replication, because the PanSci Layer-2 analysis is computed against the same Tabula Sapiens covariance used in the primary analysis. Critically, the leading within-type driver genes differ across the protocol switch: 25 of 35 cell types are ribosomal-dominated on the rank-1 CPC1 axis in the whole-cell data, versus 0 of 16 in PanSci. The nuclear rank-1 drivers are instead long, intron-rich lineage genes (for example ARHGAP15, DOCK2, MBNL1, DPYD, KCNIP4, CALCRL, SPTBN1). A statistic that clears its permutation null while its driver genes are protocol-dependent is the signature of a conserved covariance statistic whose dominant biological axis is not shared across the assay.

Length disparity in the nuclear drivers. The nuclear rank-1 CPC1 drivers are far longer than the short ribosomal-protein genes that dominate the whole-cell axis. On the tracked gene-length table, the PanSci rank-1 driver set has a median genomic span of 334,401 bp against 11,898 bp for the ribosomal-protein genes, a disparity of ≈28-fold, and a median canonical transcript length of 4,392 bp against 994 bp, ≈4.4-fold. An earlier internal analysis reported a 44-fold disparity; that figure is not reproduced from the tracked table, which gives the two multipliers above, and no argument here depends on the precise value. Because single-nucleus capture over-represents long, intron-retaining transcripts, the leading covariance axis in nuclear data tracks transcript length rather than a cell-identity program.

Variance-explained asymmetry is not part of this argument. The fraction of within-type variance carried by CPC1 differs between the two sides of the protocol switch; for B cell it is 0.083 in the whole-cell data against 0.372 in PanSci, a ≈4.5-fold asymmetry. We do not treat that as evidence of incommensurability, because asymmetries of comparable and greater magnitude, up to about 8-fold, occur between species within the whole-cell analysis itself (S6 Table). A criterion that the primary analysis fails more severely cannot disqualify the comparison it is invoked against. The incommensurability argument rests on the driver composition and the length contrast above, and on those alone.

What the same-protocol replication does and does not control. Taken together, a length-driven axis in nuclear data and a ribosomal or identity-driven axis in whole-cell data mean the two covariance bases are not commensurable across the protocol switch. The Krzanowski subspace statistic can clear its permutation null, because the subspaces overlap enough to beat a label shuffle, while the underlying biological axis is not the same axis in both assays. Clearing the null is therefore not by itself evidence of a shared axis in any pair, including the primate pairs. The basal-ganglia replication is same-protocol, and holding protocol fixed removes the assay confound from the cross-species contrast: both sides of every pair are single-nucleus 10x multiome data from one harmonized atlas. It does not place that comparison outside the nuclear regime described above. Consistent with that, in the same-protocol primate atlas the magnitude of the CPC1 gene loading correlates with gene length (pooled Spearman ρ ≈ 0.34 for canonical transcript length and ≈ 0.44 for genomic span, with per-type p < 0.05 in every type), attenuating to ρ ≈ 0.24 under per-gene standardization. Attenuation is not removal. What addresses the length component in the primate pairs is the length-residualization control reported in Methods, not the same-protocol design, and that control is where the claim of near-orthogonality to length is established.

One test is not available from the deposited statistics. Whether the leading within-type drivers are the same genes on both sides of a given primate pair cannot be answered from CPC1, which is the top eigenvector of the summed covariance and is species-invariant by construction; per-species eigenvectors would be required. What the deposited analyses establish is that the axis moves toward identity markers under standardization and is nearly orthogonal to gene length, not that the same genes lead it in both species of a pair.
