========================================================================
TITLE
========================================================================


Conserved geometry of cell-type identity: a mammalian configuration and a replicating two-layer structure in primates

Authors:
Sriram Devadas*
Independent Researcher, Acton, MA, USA

*Corresponding author: sriram@sriramdevadas.com


========================================================================
Abstract
========================================================================


The relative positions of cell types in transcriptomic space, the geometry of cell-type identity, are widely assumed to be conserved across species. But which features of that geometry are conserved, and which can be measured reliably in current data, have not been separated. We represent each cell type as a landmark, its mean expression across orthologous genes, and use Procrustes superimposition to decompose the cross-species geometry into two levels: the configuration of cell types relative to one another (Layer 1), and the orientation of each type's within-type expression spread (Layer 2). In human and mouse, the configuration is conserved far beyond chance (observed cross-species disparity 0.52 times the null median, where 1 is chance and lower is more conserved; p < 10⁻⁶). This conservation is carried disproportionately by the genes whose cross-species expression is most conserved, which are enriched for canonical identity transcription factors. The within-type covariance is conserved as well: the alignment that best fits the centroids compresses its orientation rather than absorbing it, leaving a distinct conserved residual. This decomposition replicates on three primate pairs, human, macaque, and marmoset, from a single harmonized basal-ganglia atlas (compression significant in every pair at every subspace dimension tested, all p ≤ 10⁻⁴); there, under gene-level standardization, the compressed axis moves off housekeeping structure toward cell-identity markers. Per-type divergence rankings behave differently: precise within an atlas but near-uncorrelated across atlases. In a simulation calibrated to the real data, rank recovery saturates at ρ ≈ 0.42 at the observed signal strength and does not rise as more cells are sampled, so additional cells will not resolve the rankings. Between-atlas technical variation is comparable to between-species biological variation at this resolution. Conserved cell-type geometry is measurable and replicates; per-type divergence is not yet resolvable in available atlases, a boundary that constrains how single-atlas divergence estimates should be read.


Keywords: Procrustes analysis, cross-species transcriptomics, cell type evolution, single-cell RNA-seq, cell type divergence, cell type geometry


========================================================================
Author Summary
========================================================================


Every cell type carries a characteristic pattern of active genes that places it at a particular position relative to all the others, and together those positions form an arrangement with a definite shape. That arrangement is widely assumed to be shared across animals, but two questions about it are easy to run together: is it conserved across species, and can today's data actually measure its finer features? To ask both, we borrowed a method from comparative anatomy, the geometry used to compare skulls by aligning matching landmarks, and applied it to the average expression profile of each matched cell type in human, mouse, mouse lemur, macaque, and marmoset. The arrangement is conserved, measurably more than chance, and is carried by the genes whose expression is most conserved across species, which include the master regulators of cell identity. Its two-layer structure (where each type sits, and how each type's cells spread around it) is conserved as well and replicates on independent primate data. But the popular per-type readout, the ranking of which cell types have "diverged most," cannot be recovered reliably from the atlases we have now: two atlases of the same species can disagree as much as two species do. That boundary matters for how such rankings should be read.


========================================================================
INTRODUCTION
========================================================================


Cell-type geometry, and two questions about it.

A cell type occupies a characteristic position in transcriptomic space, set by which genes it expresses and how strongly. Taken together, the cell types of an organism form an arrangement: some types close, some far, the whole with a definite shape. This arrangement is what we mean by the geometry of cell-type identity, and whether it is preserved across species is a natural way to ask whether cell-type identity itself is evolutionarily conserved.

To make the arrangement comparable across species, we borrow the framework geometric morphometrics uses to compare anatomical forms[7]. There, a form is reduced to a configuration of landmarks, corresponding points such as the same anatomical feature on two skulls, and two forms are compared by superimposing their landmarks to remove differences in position, orientation, and overall scale. What remains after superimposition is shape. We treat cell types as landmarks: each type's mean expression vector is a point, all types from both species are placed in a shared space of orthologous genes, and Procrustes superimposition aligns the two species' configurations. What remains is the conserved shape of the cell-type arrangement.

That arrangement has two levels, and we keep them separate throughout because they turn out to behave differently. Layer 1 is the configuration: where the cell-type centroids sit relative to one another. Layer 2 is the dispersion: the orientation of the cloud of cells around each centroid. For each layer we ask two questions that are easy to conflate. First, is the structure conserved between species? Second, is it recoverable from single-cell atlases as they exist today, that is, is what we measure a property of biology or of the particular atlases we measured? These are different questions, they have different answers here, and separating them is the point of this work: the conserved arrangement is real and reproducible, while one widely reported quantity, the ranking of which cell types have diverged most, turns out not to be recoverable from current data at all.

Comparative single-cell studies have developed a rich toolkit for relating cell types across species, but it answers a different question from the one above. Methods such as SAMap[2], SATURN[3], CAMEX[4], and foundation-model[6] approaches optimize cell-level matching: they establish which cell type in one species corresponds to which in another, producing correspondence scores, integrated embeddings, or transport probabilities. These are answers to "which maps to which." A separate approach quantifies cross-species divergence directly: optimal-transport formulations[5] return a distance between two species' transcriptome distributions, computed for each cell type. What none of these produces is a measure of how much a given cell type's position has shifted under a single geometric transform fit across all types at once, a per-type quantity that treats the cross-species comparison as one alignment of two configurations and reads off what each type contributes to the residual. That per-type displacement, and the conservation of the overall configuration it presupposes, is what a geometric-morphometric treatment supplies.

The geometry itself has been shown to carry biological information within a species. Bulk transcriptomes of normal cell types exhibit a tree-like organization that is degraded in cancer[1], establishing that the arrangement of cell types, not only their individual expression profiles, reflects real biology. Whether that arrangement is conserved across species, and whether its conservation can be decomposed into separable geometric components, has not been quantified with single-cell resolution, where individual cell types can be dissociated from tissue composition.

Here we address both the conservation question and the measurement question together, and find they have different answers. Applying Procrustes superimposition to ontology-matched cell-type centroids, we show that the configuration of cell types is conserved across two mammalian orders, the primate–rodent divergence, and independently within primates; that a second geometric layer, the orientation of within-type covariance, is conserved as well and replicates on an independent primate atlas; and that these conserved features are carried by conserved cell-identity genes. We then show that the per-type divergence ranking the same framework produces, though widely reported[23,24] and precise within any single atlas, is not reproducible across atlases: the limit is one of signal, not sampling. Conserved cell-type geometry is measurable and reproducible; the finer question of which cell types have diverged most is, for current data, not yet resolvable, a boundary that bears directly on how cross-species divergence estimates from single atlases should be interpreted.


========================================================================
RESULTS
========================================================================


1. The configuration of cell types is conserved across species

Across 35 cell types matched between human and mouse by Cell Ontology label, the arrangement of cell-type centroids in transcriptomic space is conserved far beyond chance. Representing each type as its mean expression across 16,959 one-to-one orthologs and aligning the two species' configurations by Procrustes superimposition, the observed cross-species disparity is roughly half that of randomized configurations (observed-to-null ratio 0.52; p < 10⁻⁶, one million permutations; Fig 1A,B). Lower ratios indicate stronger conservation, and 0.52 places the cell-type arrangement well inside the conserved regime.

Two controls establish that this reflects the biology of the arrangement rather than the way it was measured. The signal is not manufactured by the shared embedding: when each species is embedded independently and the configurations aligned without any joint basis, conservation is at least as strong (S1 Fig). And it is not merely lineage-level clustering (immune cells near immune cells, epithelial near epithelial), because a permutation test that shuffles labels only within lineage blocks, holding coarse structure fixed, still finds the observed configuration significantly ordered (obs/null 0.67; p < 10⁻⁴; Fig 1C). Cell types occupy conserved positions relative to their neighbors, not just conserved neighborhoods.

The conservation is not confined to a single atlas pair, and the mammalian reach of the result rests on the human–mouse comparison, which spans the deep primate–rodent divergence (~90 million years[8]). A second, independently generated pair (human and mouse lemur, a strepsirrhine primate separated from human by roughly 75 million years[8]) clears the same test (obs/null 0.35; p < 10⁻⁴; Fig 1D), an independent replication of the configuration signal in a different primate lineage rather than at a deeper divergence. Conserved cell-type configuration is thus a property of more than one species pair: it holds across the primate–rodent divergence (~90 Myr, two mammalian orders), the deepest split tested, and is reproduced independently within primates.

A configuration, though, is only the coarsest description of the geometry. It records where each cell type sits, but not the shape of the variation around it. Whether conservation reaches that finer structure is the next question.


2. Both geometric layers are conserved, and the decomposition replicates on an independent primate atlas

Position and dispersion are separately conserved. Beyond where each centroid sits, the configuration of Layer 1, each cell type carries a second geometric feature: the orientation of the cloud of its cells around that centroid, its covariance ellipsoid (Layer 2). Across species these orientations agree above chance, but that agreement could be nothing more than a reflection of the configuration. To test whether it is more, we apply the centroid-optimal rotation to the ellipsoids and re-measure. Were the agreement simply redundant with the centroid alignment, this rotation would absorb it and the re-measured agreement would fall to what random cell-type matching gives. It does not: the rotation compresses the agreement, Krzanowski[12] subspace similarity S drops from 0.483 to 0.230 at k = 5, but leaves it well above its null (0.180; p < 10⁻⁴; Fig 2A,B). Ellipsoid orientation is conserved beyond what the configuration accounts for. Position and dispersion are two separately-conserved properties, not one.

This second layer is estimated from individual cells, which raises a question the configuration does not: is the conserved orientation a property of the biology, or of the particular atlas in which it was measured? We tested it on an independent, same-protocol atlas, the harmonized 10x basal-ganglia atlas of the Human and Mammalian Brain Atlas consortium[25], in three cross-species pairs among human, macaque, and marmoset (52 to 55 matched cell types, a few hundred to over 100,000 cells each). The two-layer structure replicates in all three: the centroid-optimal rotation compresses the covariance agreement at every subspace dimension tested, each beyond its permutation null (all p ≤ 10⁻⁴; Fig 2C). The three pairs come from one atlas and are not independent of one another, but they reproduce the human–mouse finding in a second consortium, protocol, and tissue.

The conserved orientation is biological, not a technical artifact of expression magnitude or transcript length. Per-gene standardization removes the dominance of high-mean housekeeping genes, the ribosomal-protein driver that tops the axis in 25 of 35 human–mouse types unscaled drops to 1 of 35, and projecting the transcript-length axis out of the standardized expression leaves the compression essentially unchanged in every pair: post-rotation S shifts by at most a few percent and still clears its null at p ≤ 10⁻⁴, while the residual correlation between the axis and gene length collapses from about 0.24–0.33 to about 0.05–0.11. The length axis is nearly orthogonal to the conserved one. Under standardization the axis moves off housekeeping structure toward cell-identity markers, though a canonical marker is the single strongest driver for only a minority of types, most clearly in the closest pair, Human–Macaque (18 of 55), and fewer at greater divergence.

Same protocol is the point: a whole-cell to single-nucleus comparison would confound assay with biology, in nuclear data the leading covariance axis tracks transcript length, not identity (S2 Text), so the primate replication, not a cross-protocol pair, is the informative test of Layer 2.

The geometry is conserved at both levels and reproduces on an independent atlas. How widely the configuration itself holds, across the many atlases now available, and across greater evolutionary distances, is the next question.


3. The configuration result is robust across atlases and divergence times

The configuration signal is not specific to one atlas pair; it recurs across independently constructed atlases. Three single-source cross-species replications, a second mouse atlas, a single-nucleus atlas built on an assay independent of both original datasets, and a human harmonized meta-atlas, are each significantly conserved (obs/null 0.45–0.55, each p < 10⁻⁴; Fig 3), at magnitudes clustering around the primary. A pooled replication assembled from fifteen collections that share neither original atlas is also significantly conserved, though its heterogeneity-pooled magnitude is weaker and not on the same scale as the single-source pairs (obs/null 0.811, p < 10⁻⁴, n = 22); the signal is present even in data that share nothing with the primary.

Two attempts do not reach significance, and in both the cause is technical and identifiable rather than biological. A six-type liver atlas is underpowered by its small landmark count, though its effect size falls within the conserved range; a microwell-based atlas has low per-cell sensitivity, and a within-human control localizes that failure to the atlas rather than to the method, since human centroids from two independent atlases agree well (S2 Fig). Separately, a human–macaque comparison is consistent in direction but underpowered at twelve matched types and does not clear multiple-testing correction; it is reported in S1 Text and not interpreted further.

The configuration, then, recurs across independent atlases, and where it does not, the failure is localizable to how an atlas was built rather than to the biology. This makes the behavior of the per-type readout the same alignment produces, the ranking of which cell types have diverged most, all the more striking, because it shares none of this robustness.


4. Per-type divergence rankings are precise within an atlas but not resolvable across atlases

The same alignment that yields the conserved configuration also yields the single most-requested output of a cross-species comparison, a per-type divergence ranking, ordering cell types by how far each has moved, and it behaves nothing like the global signal. Within one atlas the ranking is strikingly reproducible: every one of the 35 types holds a stable rank, with a median 95% confidence interval of three ranks (Fig 4A), and no single pre-specified factor we tested, network centrality, sequence conservation, expression-level confounds, and others (S1 Text), explains it. Across atlases, that structure does not survive. The ranking is essentially uncorrelated between independent atlas pairs (Spearman ρ = +0.15, +0.19, −0.05, and −0.14 across four pairs; Fig 4B), and the types pinned most tightly within an atlas are, if anything, the ones that move most across them (ρ = −0.41, p = 0.073). Precision within a dataset buys nothing across datasets, the signature of a measurement reading out structure specific to the atlas it was made in.

Reproducible is not the same as accurate, and a plant-and-recover simulation separates the two. Re-sampling the same data recovers the same ranking almost perfectly (test–retest ρ = 0.994), this is the within-atlas precision, reproduced in silico. But when a known divergence ranking is planted and noise added at the signal strength of the real atlases, recovery of the true order tops out at ρ ≈ 0.42, and it stays there no matter how many cells are sampled, from 50 to 2,000 (Fig 4C). The limit is one of signal, not sampling: more cells sharpen each centroid but cannot recover an ordering the signal does not carry. Its exact value depends on how broadly true divergences are assumed to be spread; the qualitative result, that more cells do not help, does not. And the ceiling accounts for the collapse: two atlases each recovering the truth only to ρ ≈ 0.42 would agree with one another at roughly 0.42² ≈ 0.18, essentially the ≈0.15 observed. The cross-atlas disagreement is not a second problem layered on the sampling limit; it is what the sampling limit predicts.

The consequence for how these rankings should be read is concrete. Between-atlas technical variation is as large as between-species biological variation: in a scale-matched comparison, two atlases of the same species are less alike (obs/null 0.607) than two different species are (0.317; S4 Fig). A per-type divergence ranking from a single atlas pair is therefore an atlas-specific observation, not a resolved biological ordering. Whether a single true ranking exists and current data cannot resolve it, or per-type divergence is itself context-dependent, a cell type's divergence measured from one tissue mixture need not equal its divergence measured from another, the practical conclusion is identical: resolving which cell types have diverged most will require harmonized, composition-controlled designs across atlases, not deeper sequencing of the ones we have.


5. The conserved configuration is carried by conserved cell-identity genes

Having drawn that boundary, we can say what its measurable side rests on: the conserved configuration is carried by the genes whose expression is most conserved across species. Restricting the configuration to the top conservation quartile preserves the signal: a check that conserved rather than divergent genes carry it, and, against a derangement null, one that is not circular (S1 Text). The substantive result is independent of that check: these conserved genes are enriched for canonical master and identity transcription factors, above backgrounds matched for both expression level and cell-type specificity (median conservation percentile 0.94, versus 0.54 for an expression-matched and 0.76 for a jointly matched background; Fig 5, S11 Table). This grounds the geometric signal in a concrete molecular basis: the conserved arrangement is an arrangement of cell identities, encoded by the regulators that define them.

Taken together, the results draw a clean line through what current data can and cannot support. The configuration of cell types is conserved across two mammalian orders, the primate–rodent divergence of roughly 90 million years, replicates at the level of within-type covariance across primate atlases, and is carried by conserved identity genes. The per-type divergence ranking that the same framework produces, and that is widely reported, is precise within an atlas but not recoverable across atlases: the limit is one of signal, not sampling, so more cells do not resolve it. Conserved cell-type geometry is measurable; the finer question of which types have diverged most is, for now, beyond the reach of the atlases we have.


========================================================================
DISCUSSION
========================================================================


Treating cell types as landmarks and comparing species by superimposition separates two things that a single similarity score conflates: whether the arrangement of cell types is conserved, and whether the shape of variation around each type is conserved. Both are, and they are conserved as distinct properties: the alignment that best matches the centroids compresses the covariance orientation rather than absorbing it, so position and dispersion carry separable conserved information. That this two-layer structure replicates across three primate pairs from a single harmonized atlas, and that under gene-level standardization the conserved axis moves off housekeeping structure toward cell-identity markers, indicates a shared biological organization rather than a statistical coincidence of one dataset.

The more consequential result is a boundary. The per-type divergence ranking, which cell types have diverged most between species, is among the most natural quantities to extract from a cross-species comparison, and it is widely reported. We find it precise within any single atlas but not reproducible across atlases, and a simulation shows why: at the signal strength of real data, the recoverable correlation between a true divergence ranking and the estimated one saturates at ρ ≈ 0.42 and does not rise as more cells are sampled. The instability is a property of the information available in current atlases, not of sampling. In practical terms, between-atlas technical variation is comparable to between-species biological variation at this level of geometry, and a divergence ranking from a single atlas pair should be read as atlas-specific rather than as a resolved biological ordering. This does not mean cross-species divergence is unmeasurable in principle; it means that measuring it will require harmonized, multi-atlas designs that control annotation and composition across sources. It is a design constraint, not a deeper sequencer.

Three bounds delimit these conclusions. First, the covariance-layer replication is demonstrated across primates; whether it extends to greater divergence, such as the primate–rodent split at which the configuration is conserved, remains to be tested with same-protocol atlases at that distance. Second, the divergence-ranking result is a statement about currently available atlases, established on the species and tissues examined here; it identifies a resolution limit under present data rather than a permanent one. Third, the framework compares configurations of ontology-matched types and inherits the granularity of those annotations: a coarsely annotated type and a finely annotated one are not equivalent landmarks[22], which is one reason harmonized annotation is the operative requirement for resolving per-type divergence. Each of these is a boundary on scope, and none is contradicted by the results within it.

The conserved configuration is carried disproportionately by the most cross-species-conserved genes, which are enriched for master and identity transcription factors. We read this as a quantification of the identity interpretation, not as a discovery about regulation: the conserved arrangement is the arrangement of cell identities, encoded by conserved regulatory genes. The enriched genes are, in part, conserved by the same evolutionary pressures that conserve the geometry, and we do not separate those. The value of the result is that it ties an abstract geometric signal to a concrete and independently sensible molecular basis.

The two-layer decomposition is a general instrument: any pair of cell-type collections in a shared feature space can be compared this way, and the same machinery that here separates conserved configuration from conserved dispersion could compare configurations across conditions, developmental stages, or perturbations. The immediate methodological contribution, though, is the pair of findings taken together: that conserved cell-type geometry is real, replicable, and identity-bearing, and that the per-type divergence structure the same method produces is not yet resolvable in available data. Reporting both, rather than the positive alone, is what the measurement supports; it marks the line between what current single-cell atlases can establish about cell-type evolution and what they cannot yet.


========================================================================
Materials and Methods
========================================================================


Ethics statement

This study is a computational reanalysis of previously published, publicly available single-cell and single-nucleus RNA-sequencing datasets; no new human or animal data were generated. All datasets were obtained in de-identified form from public repositories, where they were deposited by the original studies under their own institutional ethical and animal-care approvals. Because only existing, publicly available, de-identified data were analyzed, no additional ethics approval was required.

The framework used throughout is landmark-based Procrustes superimposition of ontology-matched cell-type centroids, with a two-layer decomposition into configuration and within-type covariance; we refer to it as CellWarp. All analyses were implemented in Python 3.12 with a global random seed of 42; the version-pinned dependency manifest, the archived ortholog tables, and all scripts are deposited with the code (Data and code availability). Dated internal analysis plans were committed to the repository before the corresponding statistics were computed; "pre-specified" throughout denotes a design or threshold decision fixed before the corresponding result was observed. No analysis was registered with an external public pre-registration registry; some of the deposited plans cover analyses reported elsewhere or held in reserve rather than in this paper, and the full classification of every script, analysis, and plan (paper, banked, other-project, exploratory) is provided in the repository as SCOPE.md.

Data and cell-type matching

Primary human data were from Tabula Sapiens[14] (v1.0; 483,152 cells, 24 donors, 10x Chromium and Smart-seq2) and primary mouse data from Tabula Muris Senis[15] (whole-organism, multiple ages, 10x Chromium and Smart-seq2), both accessed via CZ CELLxGENE Census[18] (version 2025-11-08), which is the reproducibility anchor for these datasets. The mouse-lemur extension used Tabula Microcebus[11] (Microcebus murinus, 10x 3′ v2, accessed via CZ CELLxGENE Discover). The primate covariance replication (Layer 2) used the harmonized 10x basal-ganglia atlas of the Human and Mammalian Brain Atlas consortium (human, macaque, and marmoset). Because both atlases integrate across multiple tissues per organism, each cell-type centroid aggregates across tissue contexts rather than reflecting a single organ; because Tabula Muris Senis spans several ages whereas the human atlas is predominantly adult, an age-composition difference between the two species is not controlled. Like tissue-composition and annotation-granularity differences, this falls on the per-type residuals rather than the global configuration, and is one more reason those residuals are read here as atlas-specific rather than as resolved biological divergence[21].

Cross-species correspondence was defined operationally by shared Cell Ontology[20] label: a type was included when it appeared under an identical canonical Cell Ontology display string in both atlases and reached at least 500 cells per species after quality filtering. No marker re-annotation was applied. This treats a shared label as a correspondence, not an assertion of evolutionary homology[9]; for broad parent labels (for example stromal cell, epithelial cell, generic monocyte) it may aggregate subtypes whose cross-species correspondence is not established, so residuals at those labels confound divergence with cross-species subtype-composition differences that the framework does not separate. Under the Census filters, 66 labels were shared across the two atlases and 35 met the 500-cell threshold, spanning immune/hematopoietic, epithelial, stromal/mesenchymal, endothelial, and metabolic/parenchymal lineages (full list, S5 Table). A 6-type subset (B cell, CD4+ T cell, CD8+ T cell, endothelial cell, hepatocyte, macrophage) is used where a smaller matched n is required.

Human–mouse one-to-one orthologs were obtained from Ensembl BioMart[26] release 115 (GRCh38.p14 / GRCm39; accessed 2026-03-12); only strict 1:1 relationships were retained (paralog averaging was not performed, which deflates rather than inflates any conservation signal). Restricting to this ortholog set does not itself manufacture the conserved shape: the permutation null, computed on the same gene space, returns chance-level agreement under random cell-type matching, so the conservation reflects structure across these genes rather than their selection. After intersecting with genes present in both atlases, the operating gene space comprised 16,959 orthologs. The full ortholog table is archived as CSV and is the reproducibility anchor for all gene-space definitions. A maximum of 2,000 cells per type per species was randomly subsampled (seed 42) before centroid computation for the primary human–mouse analysis; no per-type subsampling was applied for the covariance (Layer 2) analyses, which use all available cells of each type.

The Procrustes framework

Cell-type centroids were computed as the mean of log1p-transformed, library-size-normalized (counts per 10,000; CP10K) expression over all cells of a type within a species, independently per species with no cross-species alignment, integration, or batch correction; the cross-species signal is therefore present in the raw centroid displacement, not introduced by alignment. Because centroids are means of log1p expression, high-mean ubiquitous genes (ribosomal, housekeeping) contribute disproportionately to centroid and within-type-covariance geometry, which is why the leading unscaled within-type axis is ribosomal-dominated (below). The configuration itself is not an artifact of this high-mean structure: excluding ribosomal-protein genes, or the broader set of housekeeping genes, entirely leaves it as strong or stronger (obs/null 0.501 and 0.479, versus 0.522; each p < 10⁻⁴), and per-gene standardization that equalizes all gene variances, the most stringent removal of high-mean dominance, leaves it conserved (0.606; S1 Text). High-mean genes do not manufacture the signal; if anything, removing them sharpens it. The configuration is likewise robust to how cells are aggregated into centroids: recomputing the centroids as pseudo-bulk profiles (aggregating counts per type before normalization and log transformation, rather than averaging per-cell log values) leaves the configuration clearly conserved under both an equal-cell-weighted and a library-size-weighted aggregation (obs/null 0.49 and 0.62; each p < 10⁻⁴; S1 Text).

Prior to superimposition, principal component analysis was applied jointly to the stacked matrix of 70 centroids (35 types × 2 species) in the 16,959-gene space, retaining the number of components reaching 95% cumulative variance (33 components, 95.2%). Dimensionality reduction is required for numerical stability: with only 70 landmark points in thousands of dimensions, Procrustes alignment is otherwise underdetermined. Rankings were stable across k = 10–50 (S2 Fig). Procrustes superimposition was implemented directly (not via a library routine) to expose per-type residuals in the original gene space. Each configuration was centered; the cross-covariance M = XcᵀYc of the centered human (Xc) and mouse (Yc) configurations was decomposed by singular value decomposition M = UΣVᵀ; the rotation R = VDUᵀ (D diagonal with final entry sign(det(VUᵀ)), others 1) algebraically guarantees det(R) = +1, precluding reflections; and the rotated mouse configuration was scaled by the optimal full-Procrustes scalar. Procrustes distance is the sum of squared residual displacements; per-type residuals are the Euclidean distances between aligned human–mouse centroid pairs (higher = more divergent). The custom implementation was verified against scipy.spatial.procrustes to machine precision.

Significance was assessed by a permutation null that shuffles cell-type labels (not cells within types) in the mouse configuration, testing whether the global transform aligns all types simultaneously better than chance; the empirical p-value uses the conservative (n≤ + 1)/(nperm + 1) estimator[27]. The primary human–mouse test used 1,000,000 permutations (observed distance 61.15; nearest null 98.88; zero permutations ≤ observed; p < 10⁻⁶); replications, extensions, and secondary tests used 10,000 permutations (floor p < 10⁻⁴). The observed-to-null ratio (observed distance / median permuted distance) is the effect size; because the null median varies with type count, obs/null is not comparable across analyses with different n. A lineage-stratified null (labels shuffled only within five lineage blocks) tests positional correspondence beyond lineage membership (obs/null 0.668, p < 10⁻⁴). Robustness was confirmed by bootstrap resampling (50% of cells per type; Procrustes-distance CV = 0.004 over 100 iterations) and by leave-one-out cross-validation with PCA and rotation refit per fold (35/35 held-out types predicted better than random pairing). An independent-PCA variant (per-species PCA aligned via loadings, label-free) and a Mantel test on pairwise-distance matrices are reported as artifact-ruling controls (S1 Fig; S1 Text).

The two-layer decomposition

Layer 1 is centroid position (the primary Procrustes result). For Layer 2, single cells of each type were projected into the same 33-component joint PCA space, the per-type 33×33 covariance was computed (all cells; eigendecomposition by NumPy linalg.eigh), and the leading k eigenvectors define each type's covariance-ellipsoid orientation. Cross-species alignment was quantified by a k-normalized form of the Krzanowski[12] subspace-similarity statistic, S(k) = ‖AᵀB‖F² / k (A, B the k leading eigenvectors for the two species; range 0–1, higher = better alignment) at k ∈ {1, 3, 5}, against a 10,000-permutation label-shuffle null. Unconstrained by the centroid alignment, Layer 2 was significant at k = 5 (S = 0.483 vs null 0.375; p < 10⁻⁴) and k = 3, but not k = 1 (a single axis is too narrow to detect at this n). The centroid-optimal rotation R was then applied to each type's mouse-side covariance eigenvectors and compared to the unrotated human eigenvectors: this compresses aggregate alignment from S = 0.483 to S = 0.230 at k = 5, and the compressed statistic remains above its null (0.180; p < 10⁻⁴). This post-rotation null holds R fixed at its observed value and shuffles cell-type labels, testing whether the residual agreement exceeds what matching random types under the same rotation gives; the test is therefore conditional on R, not a marginal test of Layer 2.

The leading conserved axis per type (CPC1; top eigenvector of the cell-count-weighted covariance sum) was projected back to gene space and ranked by loading. Under unscaled weighting 25 of 35 types have a ribosomal-protein rank-1 driver, a property of the unscaled covariance, not a housekeeping claim, which per-gene standardization removes (1 of 35 ribosomal); excluding ribosomal-protein genes entirely leaves the compression significant (post-rotation S = 0.234 vs null 0.180; p < 10⁻⁴). Under standardization the axis moves off housekeeping structure, but a canonical identity marker is the single strongest driver for only a minority of types (primate fractions, Fig 2C). The conserved covariance orientation is thus a real, separately-conserved property; we do not claim a single universal driver set for it, because the specific drivers depend on the molecular pool a protocol samples (whole-cell versus nuclear; S2 Text).

Primate replication (basal ganglia)

The Layer 2 decomposition was replicated on the harmonized 10x basal-ganglia atlas of the Human and Mammalian Brain Atlas consortium across three same-protocol cross-species pairs (Human–Macaque, Human–Marmoset, Macaque–Marmoset; 52 to 55 matched cell types, a few hundred to over 100,000 cells per type). The three pairs are drawn from this one atlas and are not mutually independent, but the atlas is independent of the human–mouse primary in consortium, protocol, and tissue. For each pair, centroids and per-type covariances were computed on the shared ortholog space, joint PCA was fit per pair at the 95% variance threshold, and the two-layer statistics S(k) were computed pre- and post-centroid-rotation at k ∈ {1, 3, 5} against a 10,000-permutation label-shuffle null, as in the primary analysis, under two weightings: unscaled (Scheme W0) and per-gene standardized (Scheme B / W2). In every pair, at every k, the centroid-optimal alignment compressed the covariance axis while remaining above its null (all p ≈ 10⁻⁴). Under unscaled weighting the rank-1 CPC1 driver was never a canonical identity marker; under standardization a canonical marker becomes the single strongest driver for a minority of types, 18 of 55 (Human–Macaque), 7 of 52 (Human–Marmoset), and 5 of 52 (Macaque–Marmoset), largest in the closest pair, so the axis moves off housekeeping structure toward identity without resolving to markers for most types. A driver counts as a canonical identity marker by membership in a fixed brain-marker list, applied without checking that the marker is specific to the type it drives; the fractions are therefore a coarse index of proximity to identity structure rather than a validated per-type marker rate (S1 Text).

To test directly whether the conserved axis is a shared transcript-length or length-biased-capture artifact rather than biology, the gene-length direction was projected out of each cell's per-gene-standardized expression before every within-type covariance was computed, and the full two-layer pipeline, joint-PCA basis, centroid-optimal rotation, k ∈ {1, 3, 5}, and the 10,000-permutation null re-run on the residualized data, was repeated unchanged. The length direction was the unit-norm gene-length vector in the shared ortholog space, parameterized as centered-log10 length (which carries the monotone length gradient; a raw-base-pair variant, outlier-dominated, is a robustness reference), for canonical transcript length, genomic span, and both jointly. Across all three primate pairs and the primary human–mouse pair, and all three length variants, the post-rotation compression is essentially unchanged: post-rotation S shifts by at most 0.06 (mostly ≤ 0.02) and continues to clear its null at p ≤ 10⁻⁴ wherever the un-residualized statistic did, while the residual correlation between the axis loading and gene length collapses from about 0.24–0.33 to about 0.05–0.11, confirming the direction was removed. The conserved axis is therefore nearly orthogonal to the length axis. Both parameterizations reach the same verdict, so the conclusion does not depend on the length parameterization (L2_LENGTH_RESIDUALIZATION.md, deposited with the basal-ganglia analysis).

Each type's covariance is estimated from that type's own cells, so the aggregate is weighted toward well-sampled types; the great majority are well sampled (42/55, 37/52, 39/52 types have ≥ 500 cells in both species), and restricting the aggregate to those types leaves the compression and its significance unchanged in all three pairs and both weightings (post-rotation still below pre-rotation at k = 5, all p ≈ 10⁻⁴; layer2_lowN_robustness.json). Per-pair statistics and CPC1 driver classifications are deposited with the basal-ganglia analysis (Data and code availability).

Simulation

Pipeline statistical properties were characterized by a plant-and-recover simulation. Synthetic paired configurations were built by placing n cell-type centroids in a 50-factor latent space and embedding them into a 500-gene space via a scaled Gaussian projection; a Haar-distributed orthogonal rotation simulated the cross-species transform; and per-type Gaussian noise (log-normally distributed across types, ≈25× spread) planted a known per-type divergence ranking. A single signal-strength parameter controls the noise level; it was calibrated by grid search with interpolation to reproduce the observed primary obs/null of 0.522 (calibrated signal ≈ 3.68). Global detection power was 100% at the calibrated signal across n = 15, 25, 35, with a well-calibrated null (false-positive rate 4.8% at α = 0.05). Re-sampling the same synthetic centroids gave a within-atlas test–retest of ρ = 0.994, the in-silico analog of the high within-atlas precision seen in the real data. Recovery of the planted ranking, by contrast, Spearman ρ between planted and recovered orders, saturated at ρ ≈ 0.42 at the calibrated signal and did not rise with cells sampled per type (50–2,000); it was also largely insensitive to the signal parameter itself (ρ ≈ 0.40–0.44 over signal 3.0–5.0), indicating a limit set by the recovery problem at this signal-to-spread regime rather than by cell sampling. Because the planted per-type spread is an assumption rather than a measured quantity, the exact ceiling is model-dependent; the qualitative result, that additional cells do not raise it, is not. The recovery-ceiling and detection-power panels are Fig 4C and S1 Fig; per-condition summaries in S2 Table.

Conserved-contribution and identity-gene analysis

To ask whether the conserved configuration is carried by particular genes, each gene was assigned a cross-species conservation score C: the Pearson correlation, across the 35 matched cell-type centroids, between its human and mouse expression profiles. Because Pearson correlation is mean-centered and scale-invariant, C captures the pattern of a gene's cell-type specificity rather than its overall level, and it is computed directly on the centroids, independently of the joint-PCA and Procrustes axes, so it does not presuppose the geometric result it is used to explain. Genes with a constant profile in either species (no defined correlation) were excluded, leaving 15,940 of 16,959 orthologs. The conservation score, the quartile thresholds, and the positive-control gene list described below were all fixed in a dated analysis plan before any of these checks were computed; that plan and its repository timestamp are the pre-specification of record.

Genes in the top quartile of C were taken as the conserved set and those in the bottom quartile as the divergent set (3,985 genes each). Re-running the configuration analysis on each set tests whether conservation, rather than divergence, carries the geometric signal: the conserved set is far more coherent than the divergent set (observed-to-null 0.38 versus 0.71), and more coherent than expression-matched random gene sets of the same size (0.53). This contrast is partly expected, since genes are selected on cross-species correlation, which is related to the quantity being measured; it is therefore reported as a contrast (conserved versus divergent, conserved versus matched-random) rather than as an absolute effect, and a derangement null that breaks the gene-to-geometry correspondence while preserving both marginal distributions confirms the association is not an artifact of the selection (S1 Text).

The substantive claim is independent of that geometric re-run: the most-conserved genes are enriched for the transcription factors that define cell identity. This was tested with a pre-specified positive control of 73 canonical lineage-defining and master transcription factors, fixed in the analysis plan before testing, by comparing their median conservation percentile against a null of gene sets matched to the same genes on mean expression (an equal-frequency 20-bin match). The transcription factors sit far above the matched null (median conservation percentile 0.94 versus 0.54), exceeding it in every one of one million matched draws (p < 10⁻⁶). A post-hoc robustness check, matching the null jointly on expression and cell-type specificity (a 10-by-10-bin match, not part of the pre-specified plan), gives the same conclusion against a higher bar (null median 0.76). The enrichment is therefore not explained by the transcription factors being merely highly expressed or merely cell-type-specific.

Two checks establish that C itself is a stable quantity rather than an artifact of one dataset's donors. Splitting each species' donors into two disjoint halves (each donor kept whole, 100 random splits) and recomputing C on each half, the per-gene scores agree across halves (median cross-half Spearman 0.80), and the transcription-factor enrichment recovers in all 200 half-datasets. The distribution of C across genes is a broad continuum rather than a conserved/divergent dichotomy (Hartigan dip D = 0.007). C is sensitive to assay, as expected for a correlation over expression profiles: estimates from 10x-only and Smart-seq2-only data agree only moderately (Spearman 0.59), which is why the primary analysis holds the protocol fixed.

S3 Table. CellHint rank reversal analysis identifies annotation ontology and tissue composition as systematic drivers of cross-atlas per-type divergence ranking differences.
Per-cell-type comparison of primary (Tabula Sapiens / Tabula Muris Senis) and CellHint per-type divergence rankings for 15 matched cell types, including rank reversal magnitude, tissue counts, and annotation mapping details. Three factors explain the rank reversals: (1) annotation ontology mismatch: CellHint collapses multiple subtypes into broader categories (e.g., 10+ epithelial subtypes into a single "epithelial cell" with 9,537 cells; fibroblast at 88% cardiac tissue versus the primary analysis's separate "fibroblast of cardiac tissue"); (2) tissue composition bias: CellHint neutrophils are liver-only (tissue-resident phenotype) versus multi-tissue in the primary analysis, and CellHint plasma cells are 89% intestinal (IgA-secreting); (3) tissue count correlation with rank reversal magnitude (Spearman ρ = −0.526, p = 0.044), where types drawn from fewer tissues show the largest reversals. The global CellHint Procrustes signal is atlas-robust (obs/null = 0.448, p < 10⁻⁴, n = 15), but per-type residual ranking is sensitive to centroid definition, an expected property of any centroid-based metric. Confidence categorization: LOW = |rank diff| ≥ 9 AND tissue/ontology mismatch identified; MODERATE = |rank diff| 5–8 with direct annotation mapping; HIGH = |rank diff| ≤ 4 with direct annotation mapping. CellHint Tissue Count column: tissue counts for all 15 rows (provided in the accompanying repository); these feed into the Spearman ρ = −0.526 calculation. Primary Rank columns: first column is the 35-type primary rank projected to the 15-type subset (used for Rank Difference, characterizing the as-originally-published disagreement magnitude); second is a fresh 15-type Procrustes re-run for matched-baseline comparison.

S4 Table. Progressive harmonization of CellHint ranking correlation.
Four harmonization levels were applied to the CellHint per-type ranking comparison. Level 0: both primary and CellHint Procrustes analyses were recomputed on the shared 15-type set, ensuring residuals derive from the same-dimensional PCA space (ρ = −0.139, p = 0.621, n = 15). Level 1: "T cell" excluded due to heterogeneous tissue composition across atlases (ρ = −0.037, p = 0.899, n = 14). Level 2: remaining types restricted to shared tissues (ρ = −0.042, p = 0.897, n = 12). Level 3: cell counts capped at minimum across atlases (no change; primary counts already below CellHint counts after tissue restriction; seeds 1–4 confirmed zero variance). The original ρ = −0.386 (reported in S3 Table) was inflated by comparing residuals from incompatible PCA spaces (35-type vs 15-type). After harmonization, the correlation is near zero, indicating noisy but not systematically inverted per-type rankings.

S10 Table. Marker-similarity-stratified permutation null: coherence beyond marker-based matching.
